It’s that time of year again: graduate admissions season.

Every year, I receive many emails from prospective graduate students interested in joining my lab. Some catch my attention. Many do not.

The difference is not fancy formatting or perfect prose. It is much simpler: Has the student made a convincing case that they are genuinely interested in my lab? Is there something in their email that suggests they could succeed in a graduate program? Do they show the potential to conduct independent research?

We professors get it. Students are trying to avoid mounting application costs while also looking for an edge in a competitive admissions process.

This matters even more in the age of AI. It has never been easier to generate a polished email that sounds professional while saying almost nothing.

So, how should you email a professor about a graduate position? Here are my answers to some common questions.

Do professors actually read these emails?

Yes. At least I do.

But professors receive a lot of email, and prospective-student emails are competing for attention with everything else in the inbox.

A professor's email time is limited.  Every minute on email takes away from their own students.

You don’t need a clever subject line or an elaborate introduction. You need to quickly give me a reason to keep reading.

What should I do before emailing?

Do your homework.

Start with the professor’s webpage. What does their lab work on? Are they accepting students? Have they provided instructions for prospective students? What have they published recently?

A professor’s email is not a general information centre.

If the answer to your question is prominently displayed on my webpage, asking me the same question in an email is not a great first impression.

Should I email lots of professors?

There is nothing wrong with contacting multiple professors. You are looking for a graduate supervisor, and finding a good match matters.

But there is a difference between contacting several professors whose research genuinely interests you and sending the same email to 100 faculty members.

Professors can tell.

You are not trying to maximize the number of emails you send. You are trying to maximize the chance that the right professor reads your email and thinks:

This person might be a good fit for my lab.

How personalized should the email be?

Personalized does not mean:

Dear Professor [NAME],
I am deeply impressed by your groundbreaking research in [RESEARCH AREA].

Changing two fields in a template is not personalization.

Tell me why you are contacting me.

Maybe one of my recent papers connects to a project you worked on. Maybe my lab works on a problem you have been thinking about. Maybe you have experience that would be particularly relevant to something we are doing.

You don’t need a paragraph praising my research. One specific, thoughtful sentence can be much more effective.

Should I mention one of the professor’s papers?

If it genuinely interested you, absolutely.

But don’t just tell me that you read it.

Tell me something about it.

What did you find interesting? How does it connect to your previous work? Did it make you think about a related problem?

“I read your paper on X and found it fascinating” tells me very little.

A specific observation tells me that you actually engaged with the work.

What should I tell the professor about myself?

Give me the information that helps me answer one question:

Why might this person be a good researcher in my lab?

That could include research experience, a thesis, an interesting project, publications, relevant industry experience, open-source contributions, or other evidence that you can tackle difficult problems.

You don’t need to tell me everything you have ever done.

This is an email, not your autobiography.

Highlight the things that matter for this particular lab and let your CV provide the details.

How long should the email be?

Short.

A professor should be able to understand three things very quickly:

Who are you?

Why are you contacting me?

Why might there be a research fit?

If answering those questions requires scrolling through several screens of text, your email is probably too long.

Respect the reader’s time.

Should I attach my CV?

Yes, unless the professor says otherwise.

Make it easy for me to learn more about you.

If you have a personal webpage, Google Scholar profile, GitHub repository, or other relevant work, include links where appropriate.

Don’t make me hunt for information.

What about my grades?

Strong grades can certainly help, particularly when they are in relevant courses.

But graduate research is not simply an extension of taking courses. I am also looking for evidence of curiosity, independence, technical ability, persistence, and the ability to think about open-ended problems.

Your email should help me see the researcher behind the transcript.

Can I use AI to write the email?

Of course.

But be careful.

AI has made it incredibly easy to produce emails that are grammatically perfect, professionally worded, and completely forgettable.

In fact, polished generic writing is becoming less informative precisely because it is now so cheap to produce.

Use AI to help you communicate your ideas clearly. Don’t use it to manufacture interest you don’t have or understanding you haven’t developed.

AI can improve your writing. It cannot do your homework for you.

The strongest signal in your email is increasingly not polish.

It is specificity.

Should I send a follow-up if I don’t hear back?

One polite follow-up is reasonable.

After that, move on.

Professors are busy. They may not have funding. They may already have enough students. They may be travelling. They may simply have missed your email.

And sometimes they are not interested.

No response is frustrating, but repeatedly emailing is unlikely to change the outcome.

What am I actually trying to accomplish with this email?

This is important.

Your goal is probably not to convince a professor to admit you based on one email.

Your goal is to make them curious enough to take the next step.

Open your CV.

Click on your webpage.

Look at your research.

Reply to your email.

Perhaps arrange a conversation.

Think of the email as opening a door, not closing a deal.

So what does a good email look like?

There is no magic template, and that is partly the point.

A good email is short, specific, and genuine. It shows that you know who you are writing to. It gives the professor enough information to understand why there might be a match. And it makes it easy to learn more about you.

Don’t obsess over finding the perfect words.

Do your homework. Keep it short. Make it personal. Give the professor a reason to want to know more.

You may only get one shot at catching their attention.

Make it count.
